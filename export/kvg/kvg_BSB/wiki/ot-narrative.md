# OT narrative and dialogue

How the human translators of Ruth and Jonah (eBible kvg, 2015) render Hebrew narrative and
dialogue. These are the only OT books in Kuni, so this page is the model for every further
narrative book (Genesis, Samuel, Kings). Figures: `_eval/local/human_ruth_analysis.md`, section 2.
Grammar details are on the grammar pages; this page is about rhythm and choices.

## Opening a book and a scene

- A book opens with **"Qàgoám."** as a sentence of its own (RUT 1:1, JON 1:1), then the time
  setting: "Qàgoám. Ta khøuwa-te ndøgo, manqa ovøyam megetapak Izlael manqei gimbo-kewagam,
  vikia sekeqape ndømát." Use it once per book (or for a new story), not for every וַיְהִי.
- A scene opens with "Khøuwa nøme," (one day, RUT 3:1; also JON 1:1 where the Hebrew has
  none), "Yaq ta lova ndøgo," (that night, RUT 3:6), "Ta khøuwa-te ndøgo," (in those days).
- "The word of the LORD came to Jonah" is told as an act of speaking: "Yaq Evezøza Ziona
  manqat nqawá genømbo-eín," (and the LORD spoke to Jonah again, JON 3:1). "saying" is not
  rendered; the verse ends in a comma and the quote follows.

## Wayyiqtol chains

Hebrew narrative is a chain of וַיִּקְטֹל clauses. Kuni tells it in short sentences, almost
each opened by **Yaq**, the verb last. Inside a sentence, actions of the same subject chain
with **vø̄-** (no person marking) and the chain often closes with **sasa nd(ø)-**:

> Yaq Ziona até gendego, fia vø̄go, vø̄uketao, sasa ndøteit. (JON 1:3: So he paid the fare
> and went aboard to sail.)

> Yaq Boaz até gendego, Lut vø̄-okɨ, venanqei nonqo vø̄tøkuanem. (RUT 4:13)

"até gendego," (he for his part, then he) brings a known participant back to act (JON 1:1,
1:3; RUT 3:15, 4:1, 4:13); for a woman "até gundigu" (RUT 1:14), for a group "até gindigonem"
(JON 1:15, 1:16).

### Tail-head linkage

The human links events by repeating the last one as a background clause, often where the
Hebrew has no such clause:

> ... Moab manqei-te. Yaq Moab manqei-te giyagam, Elimelek gènaním. (RUT 1:2-3: ... in Moab.
> And while they lived in Moab, Elimelech died.)

> ... atema Betliem vemiav-te vømø̄fakhaz. Yaq Betliem vemiav-te gimøfakhaz, ... (RUT 1:19)

> Naomi nqanek manqat gu-ein, yaq ndigu nangó ginø-eivám. (RUT 1:14: Naomi said this, and
> they wept again.)

> Yaq Lut nqanek manqat gu-ewag, gundu-ein, ... (RUT 3:5: Ruth heard this and replied)

Use it at a scene break or after a speech, where English would just say "then".

### Verse-internal order

- Company, place and time often follow the verb: "Yaq ngenek ezoqam Moab manqei-té genøyág,
  zas neka nakhei angana menas namba." (RUT 1:1) — see [word order](grammar/word-order.md).
- The human reorders inside a verse to follow time and cause: in RUT 1:1 the famine is
  stated, explained ("Zapa ndǿgo, Zuda manqei-te ndøgo, lou mbaín.") and restated at the end;
  in JON 1:16 the fear comes from seeing ("Evezøza-qa bazaføgakh giqeivim, ndigu møe kandambá
  gigonem").
- Two parallel Hebrew clauses may become one: "the LORD has testified against me, the
  Almighty has afflicted me" = "Yawe noqa yage mø̀ndønø-ngiú" (RUT 1:21).
- A Hebrew verse can also be split across Kuni verse numbers: JON 1:10 opens with the end
  of Jonah's answer from 1:9.

## Dialogue

- **Frames are short.** Most turns are "Yaq <speaker> gumbo-ein / gundu-ein, “…”". A reply
  takes the hither form: gundu-ein (she), gendo-ein (he), gindu-einim (they). The long frame
  "āv ... nqǽgo" marks questions ("āv gembøebeváp nqǽgo", RUT 2:5), prayers ("āv
  gene-guligulím nqǽgo", JON 4:2), orders and formal speech ("āv gezømbe-eín nqǽgo", RUT 4:9).
- **Vocative first**, as a sentence: "Sǽ-pakha." (my daughter), "Khanánqa." (sir),
  "Evézøza." (O LORD, accent moved to the first syllable).
- **Interjections** are added: "Yáiyé!" (RUT 2:20, 4:17), "Qanimáv." (good, RUT 3:5, 4:4,
  4:11), "Nqáe!" (look), "Áe," (there!, RUT 3:8). See [speech](grammar/speech.md).
- **Rhetorical questions** become statements when the answer is obvious: "should I not seek
  a resting place for you?" = "No anganeam é-bataqa-omét." (I will find you a husband, RUT 3:1);
  "why have I found favour ...?" = "No manqei vini-ák. Geté qo tanakh kandambá qoqonøgo."
  (RUT 2:10). Real questions keep "Gê," or a question word and the circumflex.
- **Speech in speech** takes ‘ ’ (RUT 2:7, 2:21, 3:17).
- **Oaths and curses** become promises: "No Yawe-qá bugug-té qataqambø-manqaté, ..." (I promise
  before the LORD, RUT 3:13); "May the LORD punish me ... if" = "No manqat av nqate-manqate,
  matanapøteav tøgoat, yaq Yawe kopømbaqapé. Bèngiænó." (RUT 1:17).

## Making things explicit

The translators add what a Kuni reader needs (feature `explicitation`):

- the meaning of names: "Taqa manqa mokho, ‘Yage qanimāv’." (RUT 1:20);
- an unfamiliar thing, in brackets at the end of the verse: "(Bali ndigu loge gigiáp. Tiqa
  bugug andé eketóp.)" (barley, RUT 1:22);
- background: the law behind the redeemer, quoted from Moses (RUT 2:20); the gate as the
  place "angana ma manqat gi꞉vøovumam" (where the men discuss matters, RUT 4:1);
- measures in kilograms: "Ande āv 10 kilogrém ndægó." (RUT 2:17, 3:15);
- procedures: lots as "let us write the names on little papers, put them in a bag and see
  whose name comes out" (JON 1:7);
- the point of a request: "spread your garment over me" + "No ambá qonøkeɨ́n." (please marry
  me, RUT 3:9).

Images go to their meaning (feature `image_to_meaning`): "under whose wings you have taken
refuge" = "qo tøke-qa qoqotøndoqav te-te" (you came to him for help, RUT 2:12); "cannot tell
right from left" = "do not know which way is good and which is bad" (JON 4:11).

A draft may do the same, but each addition goes into the chapter notes for the team.

## Narrator's notes and genealogies

- A custom: "Ta khøuwa-te ndøgo, Izlael manqei-te, matev āv qazømbe-goám nqǽgo." + the case
  with the habitual suffix -ɨn + "Nqanek Izlael ezoqam-qá matév." (RUT 4:7). See
  [verbs](grammar/verbs.md) for -ɨn.
- Genealogy: "Yaq <X> gemanaz, <Y> vø̄qan." each link with Yaq (RUT 4:19-22); the first link
  "Pelez gemanaz, Ezlon ndøqán." The heading is recast around the line's goal: "Deivid-qa zapa
  av nqǽgo qande-itán: Tege atanakha-zapa Peléz." (RUT 4:18). The newer NT writes gendomanaz
  without Yaq (Mat 1).

> Yaq as in Ruth (2015), or gendomanaz as in the revised Matthew 1?

The team (T3, 26.10.2026): either expression can be used, with a similar meaning (term:fathered.team).

## Poetry inside narrative

Jonah's psalm (JON 2:3-10) is laid out as `//1` lines, one or two Hebrew half-verses per line,
introduced by "Geguligulim," (he prayed). It keeps the narrative's short sentences and Yaq.

## The creation account (Genesis 1): the team's refrains

Our Genesis 1 test draft (gen001) built the four refrains from the NT. The Kuni team's Genesis
(15.10.2026) has its own; the store now holds the team's forms (the draft's are superseded):

| וַיְהִי־כֵן (and it was so) | Yaq matev āv tønefakhán. | Yaq ate av-té qafakhan. |

| וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר (evening, morning) | Yaq qapøis, vøndōpave. | Yaq qakhagus ndøgo, qanaqanus qandopave. |
| יוֹם שֵׁנִי (the second day) | Ndakin khøuwa gè-menás. | Khøuwa mø̀ndømø-menas-éz. |

The team links the days with "Taqa zita-te," (after that, 1:6, 1:9, 1:11 …), not with Yaq. They
do not name the sun and moon in 1:16 ("Kandambaqape ndøgo … khapelav-qase ndøgo", the big one,
the small one), which answers our draft's question. The blessing is "Vø̀mønøzumít" (be
fruitful) and "bawan bezø̄-khouwev" (let your people grow, 1:28). The full list is on
[Genesis 1–30](genesis.md).

## Genesis narrative (Gen 1–30, the team)

Genesis is the model for Genesis 31–50 and for OT narrative in general; where it differs from
Ruth, follow Genesis. Counts are verses of Gen 1–30 (871).

- **Scenes** open with "Khøuwa nøme," (one day, 22; formula:it_came_to_pass.g); "after these
  things" is "Taqa zita-te, khøuwa nøme," (15:1, 22:1, 22:20); "Taqa zita-te" alone (after that)
  42 times. A time clause: "Ta khøuwa-te ndøgo, …" (12:4).
- **Speech**: the long frame "āv g…-eín nqǽgo" 64 times, gembo-ein 44, gezø-ein 18, gumbo-ein
  13, answers with -qavøi- 52 (speech_frame.g). "Here I am" = "No nqǽya." (22:1, 22:11).
  "Behold" = "Nqáe!" (65).
- **Looking**: "bøi ge-eqa, nqáe! …" (he looked up: behold, 18:2, 22:13); "rose early" =
  "Khøuwa nøme qandopave, qæwaemat," (21:14, 22:3).
- **Family**: "she conceived and bore a son" = "kha viniv ndøndáp, nakhasa anganeam vømbō-fakhan"
  (29:32; 19 verses with kha viniv); "she named him X" = "Yaq ndugu iz X mbo-akhá." + the meaning
  in brackets (31 times, feature name_meaning_brackets); "took her as his wife" = "vø̄-okɨ,
  vø̄-tegu-sævam-ev" (24:67).
- **Genealogies**: "X neka tegi zi, tiqa emanqat nqánek:" (this is the account of X, 5:1, 11:10,
  11:27); "X viav N qamømbo-ndap, nakhasa anganeam tèmbøe-fakhán, Y." (when X was N years old he
  fathered Y, 17 times); "Y geqan, taqa zita-te, X viav N … nakhei angana neka nakhei sakheis
  nøme vømbō-fakhaz." (5:4); the end "Yaq até khæ ndønaním." (and then he died, 5:5, 9 times);
  "gathered to his people" = "ekeza ezoqam zøpaév" (25:8, 25:17). Short lists: "X, ndego Y-gé
  eve" (10:24) or Ruth's "X gemanaz, Y vø̄qan" (10:3, 7 times). Numbers: [counting](grammar/existence-naming-counting.md).
- **Oaths and blessings**: "manqa mbusa qonǿvé" (swear to me, 21:23), "manqa mbusa nøkeza
  iz-té qatøveét" (by myself I swear, 22:16); a covenant is *poev* made true (unimanqatin-,
  9:9, 15:18); blessings with khakheinøqav- and "zenda … ve" ([Genesis 1–30](genesis.md)).
- **Greeting and grief**: bowed down "gèkhafutáv, tiqa megemege-te. Bugug até manqei-té." (18:2,
  19:1); kissed *ngenøt-* (27:27, 29:11); wept *vin-*, aloud "Eiv gèmbøe-eqavát" (27:38).
- **Explaining**: brackets in 36 verses; 1:2 and 12:10 expanded (feature explanation_in_text).
  This partly answers the question above: Genesis explains as much as Ruth does.

> **Question for the Kuni team:** Genesis lists use both "X gemanaz, Y vø̄qan" (10:3, as Ruth)
> and "X, ndego Y-gé eve" (10:24). Is there a difference the drafts should keep?
